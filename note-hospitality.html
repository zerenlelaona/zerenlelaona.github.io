A five-room hospitality business in rural Spain. Full through the summer, thin for the rest of the year, and until recently every single booking arrived through one third-party platform. There is no finance team. There is an owner who needs to know what is working, and me.

The problem · One channel, two fees

The platform charges the owner a commission on each booking. Separately, it charges the guest a fee of its own on top of the nightly price. So the price a guest sees is meaningfully higher than what the owner actually receives, and the gap between those two numbers is not the owner's to keep.

That gap is the whole opportunity, and it is worth being precise about why. A direct booking does not make the owner's commission disappear in any dramatic way. What disappears is the fee the guest was paying on top. The same room can therefore be cheaper for the guest at no cost to the owner. That is pricing room that already existed; it just had nowhere to go.

What I built

A direct booking and payment channel on the business's own website, so a guest can book and pay without going through the platform. I built the booking and payment flow; a developer worked on the wider site. Moving demand to a direct channel is a pricing problem before it is a technology problem, so the rate structure came with it: seasonal tiers, higher in summer and lower in winter, two-night minimum stays, and bundles for booking further ahead. The owner and I agreed that structure together, and it is what the business uses today.

The reporting the season runs on

Every month I produce the reporting the owner actually uses: occupancy, revenue per booking, seasonality, and what each booking channel costs once the platform's cut is counted. It is not elaborate. It is the smallest set of numbers that answers whether the season is going the way we thought it would.

The decision that changed

Summer is not the problem. Low season is. So the question became who is actually in the area outside the peak months, and cycling visitors were the obvious answer: the terrain suits it, and the cycling season runs when the beach season does not.

I proposed treating marketing spend as a cost per booking acquired rather than as general awareness spending, with a payback we could check afterwards. On that basis the owner moved budget away from local advertising and towards digital and the cycling audience. I do not approve that spend; I make the case for it, the owner decides, and then I manage it.

What is still open

The forward revenue and occupancy plan for the coming season, and the marketing budget that sits behind it, are both in progress. So is moving payment processing to a cheaper provider, which the owner has agreed to but which is not finished. The direct channel launched recently, so there is no measured shift in booking mix yet, and I am not going to claim one.

Every question turned out to be the same question in different clothes: what does this actually cost us, and what do we get back.

Why I put this on the site

It is the smallest business I have worked with and the one where the distance between an analysis and a decision is shortest. There is no committee, no deck that goes into a drawer. You work something out, you explain it to the person whose money it is, and by the next week it is either running or it is not.
