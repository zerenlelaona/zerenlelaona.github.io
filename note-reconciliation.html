The most useful automation I have built was not clever. It was boring, and that is exactly why it worked. At Goldman Sachs Asset Management our team ran a daily fund-price reconciliation: two data sources, thousands of rows, checked by hand. Manual, repetitive, slow. That was time the team could have spent on the exceptions that actually needed judgment.

Step 01 · Prove it cheap

The real insight was not technical. It was noticing that a process everyone accepted as manual did not have to be. Step one was not code: I rebuilt the check in Excel first, the tool everyone on the desk already trusted. That proof of concept alone halved the processing time. More importantly, it bought something no pitch deck can buy: the team's confidence that the idea worked, using numbers they could verify themselves.

Step 02 · Build it properly

Version two replaced the manual matching entirely. SQL pulls join the two sources on ISIN and fund code; Python compares the prices and flags any break that exceeds a basis-point tolerance. Nobody scans thousands of rows anymore. The reviewer starts from a short list of flagged exceptions, and the judgment stays where it belongs: with a human.

Illustrative. The machine does the scanning; people keep the judgment. A public demo repo with synthetic data is on GitHub.

Step 03 · What it caught

The automation surfaced around a dozen pricing breaks that manual scanning could plausibly have missed. Each one was a small data-quality story: a stale price here, a mismatched identifier there. None of them was dramatic on its own. All of them were exactly the kind of thing you want a machine to find before a client does.

Step 04 · Make it outlive you

Before I left, I wrote the manual. Automation that dies when its author leaves is not automation; it is a stunt. The process, the logic, and the failure modes are documented, and the team could run it without me from day one.

It was not complicated. Sequencing beats sophistication.

The lesson

Prove it cheap, in a tool people already trust. Build it properly, once the idea has earned the investment. Write it down, so it survives you. Most process automation fails at step one, not because the code is hard, but because nobody bothered to make the case in a language the team already believed.
