Here's the thing I took away from a year of thesis work: a company's capital structure doesn't just sit on the right-hand side of the balance sheet: it quietly decides what the company builds next. For my MSc thesis at Rotterdam School of Management I analysed 2,355 listed European firms across 2014–2024, asking one question: how does capital structure influence corporate investment decisions?

Finding 01 · Debt overhang is real

The clearest result: firms carrying higher book leverage invest materially less in CapEx. Economists call this debt overhang, an idea Stewart Myers formalised back in 1977. The plain version: when a company already owes a lot, part of the payoff from every new project effectively belongs to existing creditors before shareholders see a cent. So owners and managers quietly shelve projects that a debt-free version of the same company would fund. The textbook says this should happen. Ten years of European data say it does.

Fig. 1 · Why leveraged firms shelve good projects (conceptual)

Illustrative, not to scale: the more of a project's payoff that existing debt effectively claims, the weaker the owners' incentive to fund it. That is why leveraged firms systematically underinvest.

The textbook says it should happen. Ten years of European data say it does.

Finding 02 · R&D plays by different rules

The result that surprised me was R&D. I expected the financing mix to drive it the same way. It doesn't. R&D tracks cash-flow volatility far more than leverage: firms protect their research pipeline when cash flows are steady and cut it when they turn jumpy, almost regardless of how the balance sheet is financed. Equity issuance alone doesn't lift R&D either: companies issue shares when the market is receptive, not when the lab needs money. The timing of the raise and the timing of the investment are two different decisions.

The method · Untangling the chicken and egg

The part of the thesis I am genuinely proud of is the estimation choice. Leverage and investment cause each other: how much a firm borrows shapes what it can build, but what it plans to build also shapes how much it borrows. Run a naive regression on that and you cannot tell which direction you are measuring. It is a classic chicken-and-egg problem, and it is the reason "more debt, less CapEx" could just as easily be "less CapEx, so different debt". I estimated the model with GMM (generalised method of moments), the standard tool for dynamic panels with exactly this kind of two-way causality, so the leverage effect I report is measured against that problem rather than pretending it does not exist.

So what · For anyone who builds models

Why does this matter outside a university library? If you value companies, it changes where capital structure lives in your model. Leverage isn't just a WACC input below the line. It belongs in the forecast itself. A heavily levered target won't deliver the CapEx path of its unlevered peer, and if your projection assumes it will, the DCF is quietly optimistic. The same logic reaches FP&A: when cash flows are volatile, defending the R&D budget is a real annual fight, not a given, and a good analyst plans for that fight.

The fine print

The usual honesty applies. Even with GMM doing the heavy lifting on causality, I'd call these strong, consistent patterns rather than court-proof causation, and book leverage is a blunt measure. But the pattern holds across ten years and more than two thousand firms, which is a stronger footing than most modelling assumptions ever get.

The lesson

The lesson I carry into every valuation since: read the balance sheet as a constraint on the future, not a record of the past. The debt a company carries today is already deciding what it won't build tomorrow.
